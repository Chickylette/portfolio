TeamFight on Tiles

TeamFight on Tiles is a hex-grid auto-battler inspired by Teamfight Tactics, with an additional focus on terrain-based strategy. While retaining the core concept of positioning units and letting them battle automatically, the game introduces different tile types, each with its own movement cost.

These terrain variations influence unit movement and pathfinding, adding an extra layer of strategy to unit positioning and battlefield navigation. Players must consider not only their team composition but also how the terrain affects combat.

Hex Gird

The game features a 45-tile hexagonal grid arranged across seven rows, with each row containing six to seven tiles. The board size was determined through multiple prototypes to provide sufficient space for unit movement and strategic positioning without making the battlefield feel too sparse. The grid is divided into different zones, with the three rows closest to the player designated for unit placement and the three furthest rows used for enemy spawning. This layout creates a clear separation between opposing teams while leaving space for units to navigate and engage in combat.

Terrain

To introduce additional strategic depth, the grid features four terrain types: Road, Grass, Water, and Mountain, each represented by a different colour. These terrain types influence unit movement and pathfinding, with certain tiles allowing normal movement while others restrict movement entirely. Each terrain type has its own movement properties, affecting how units navigate across the battlefield. Impassable tiles cannot be traversed.

Pathfinding Behaviour

The flowchart below illustrates how units automatically locate, approach, and attack enemies during combat. Each unit searches for a target and checks whether it is within attack range. If the target is out of range, the unit uses the A* pathfinding algorithm to calculate a route towards a neighbouring tile of the target, taking terrain movement costs and obstacles into account.

After moving to the next tile, the unit checks its attack range again and recalculates its path if the target has moved. This process continues until the target is defeated, at which point the unit searches for a new enemy and repeats the cycle.

                            A([Search for Enemy]) --> B[Select Target]
                            B --> C{Target in Attack Range?}

                            C -->|Yes| D[Attack Target]
                            C -->|No| E[Calculate A* Path]

                            E --> F[Move to Next Tile]
                            F --> G{Target in Attack Range?}

                            G -->|Yes| D
                            G -->|No| H{Target Moved?}

                            H -->|No| I[Continue Along Path]
                            I --> F
                            D --> J{Target Defeated?}
                            J -->|Yes| A
                            J -->|No| C

Combat

Unit Types & Roles

Unit drafting is one of the core mechanics of TeamFight on Tiles. To maintain a manageable development scope while providing strategic variety, the game features a roster of approximately ten units, divided into four distinct categories: Frontliner, Backliner, Tank, and Special.

Each category has a defined combat role and set of mechanics, allowing players to make informed drafting decisions and develop different team compositions.

Unit Type Combat Range MP Generation Role & Behaviour
Frontliner Close-range Attacking Offensive melee units that penetrate enemy formations and pressure Backliners, disrupting rear-positioned damage dealers. Fighters belong to this category.
Backliner Long-range Attacking Ranged damage dealers that provide sustained damage from a safe distance, primarily targeting enemy Tanks and frontline units.
Tank Close-Range Attacking and receiving damage High-HP, low-damage units designed to absorb attacks and draw enemy aggro, protecting allied damage dealers.
Special Varies Varies by unit Units featuring unique mechanics or conditional behaviours that introduce alternative strategies and situational advantages.

Unit Rarity

A unit rarity system was introduced to distinguish unit strength and support progression throughout the game. Units are divided into three tiers: Common (C), Uncommon (UC), and Rare (R).

Each rarity serves a different purpose at various stages of gameplay, allowing players to gradually build stronger team compositions through unit drafting.

Rarity Classification

Rarity Gameplay Stage Design Purpose Visual Design
Common (C) Early Game Starting units such as Fighter, Ranger, and Tank. Effective during the first five rounds, but gradually become less impactful in later stages. Simple polygonal meshes provided within Unreal Engine.
Uncommon (UC) Mid to Late Game Form the primary combat force, providing increased strength to handle more advanced enemy waves while maintaining strategic balance. More specialised polygonal forms that distinguish them from Common units.
Rare (R) Late Game Exceptionally powerful units featuring unique abilities or game-changing mechanics. Designed to provide significant advantages in late-game encounters. Distinctive designs inspired by games and anime, emphasising their power, rarity, and unique identity.

Reward Distribution

Unit rarity probabilities change as the game progresses. Early reward rounds prioritise Common units to provide players with accessible starting options. Uncommon units become more prominent during the mid-game, while Rare units are gradually introduced in later rounds.

Round Common (C) Uncommon (UC) Rare (R)

Design Rationale

The reward distribution is designed to align unit availability with expected player progression. Common units provide a stable foundation during the early game, while Uncommon units support the transition towards more challenging encounters.

As the game advances, the increasing probability of obtaining Rare units introduces high-impact drafting opportunities. This progression encourages strategic team-building while preventing players from becoming overly powerful during the early stages or trivialising late-game challenges.
